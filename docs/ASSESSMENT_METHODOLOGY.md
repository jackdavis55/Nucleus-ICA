# NUCLEUS — Assessment Methodology

## Purpose

This document describes the conceptual sources informing the assessment. It is a design synthesis, not a claim that the application reproduces any one author's complete model.

## 1. James Reason

Reason's work should inform two separate parts of the application:

1. Human performance/error classification: distinguish skill-based slips and lapses from rule-based and knowledge-based mistakes, and consider violations and rule-related behaviour.
2. Culpability/accountability reasoning: use structured questions to examine intent, procedures, training, substitution and context.

The application must not simply digitise a decision tree and treat its output as objective truth. Each question should be interpreted alongside evidence and the actual operating context.

Important design implications:
- Assess each specific unsafe act or omission, not only the incident as a whole.
- Distinguish intended action from intended consequence.
- Examine whether procedures were available, accurate, workable, intelligible and genuinely expected to be followed.
- Use the substitution test carefully and avoid hindsight bias.
- Distinguish human error from deliberate deviation.
- Examine system-induced conditions and organisational contributors.

## 2. Sidney Dekker

Dekker's critique should inform the application design in three ways:

- Culpability boundaries are interpreted by people and organisations; they are not mechanically discovered by a decision tree.
- The process must make visible who is making the judgement, what assumptions are being made, whose account is heard and what evidence supports the conclusion.
- The individual's actual freedom, knowledge and control in context matter.

The application therefore includes a dedicated volition/personal-control stage. The MVP uses practical assessment lenses:
- Choice and constraints.
- Understanding and foreseeability.
- Control and causal influence.

These lenses are an operational design synthesis. Verify exact terminology and attribution against the chosen Dekker edition/article before describing them as a formal three-part model.

## 3. Nuclear and high-hazard human performance

Use human performance guidance to prompt examination of:
- Task demands and complexity.
- Individual capabilities and experience.
- Work environment and time pressure.
- Procedure quality and usability.
- Communication and supervision.
- Organisational priorities and culture.
- Safety barriers and defences.

The factor library should be configurable and should not be treated as exhaustive.

## 4. Just Culture response

Keep two different questions separate:

A. What type of human performance behaviour occurred?
Examples: slip, lapse, mistake, violation.

B. What organisational response is appropriate?
Examples: learning/support, coaching, system correction, independent accountability review.

A violation is not automatically reckless. A deliberate action is not automatically a freely chosen action. An adverse outcome does not prove culpability. A lack of harm does not prove the behaviour was safe.

## 5. No numerical culpability score

Do not total answers, error traps, mitigating factors or aggravating factors into a score or percentage. Factors differ in significance and must be reasoned about in context.

## 6. Evidence and uncertainty

Every conclusion should:
- Identify its evidence basis.
- Identify contrary evidence.
- State uncertainty and limitations.
- Separate fact from interpretation.
- Remain editable by the assessor.
- Be open to independent review in a later governed deployment.

Unknown is not equivalent to no. Absence of evidence is not proof that a factor was absent.

## 7. Review before operational use

Before deployment with real cases, the methodology must be reviewed by appropriately qualified nuclear operations, human factors, HR/employment, legal, information security and regulatory specialists. Validate the decision categories against the organisation's approved Just Culture policy and applicable jurisdiction.
